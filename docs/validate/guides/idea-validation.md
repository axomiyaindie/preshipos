# How to Validate a Product Idea

A promising idea is not automatically a viable product.

Before building, investigate whether a real audience experiences the
problem, whether existing alternatives solve it adequately, and whether
people have a reason to adopt another solution.

This guide provides a practical starting framework.

## 1. Define the problem

Describe the problem without describing your proposed solution.

Answer:

- Who experiences this problem?
- What are they trying to accomplish?
- What makes the current process difficult?
- How frequently does the problem occur?
- What happens if they do nothing?

**Expected outcome:** A specific problem statement for a recognizable
audience.

## 2. Identify your target audience

Avoid defining your audience as everyone who might use your product.

Identify:

- Primary user group.
- Their role or situation.
- Their current workflow.
- Their existing tools.
- Their biggest frustrations.

**Expected outcome:** A clearly defined initial audience.

## 3. Research existing alternatives

Look for direct competitors and indirect alternatives.

Investigate:

- Existing products.
- Open-source projects.
- Manual processes.
- Spreadsheets and workarounds.
- Community recommendations.

Compare their audience, core capabilities, pricing, strengths and
limitations.

Do not assume that a lack of direct competitors proves demand.

**Expected outcome:** An evidence-based competitor overview.

## 4. Investigate demand

Look for evidence that people actively experience the problem.

Useful research activities include:

- Interviewing potential users.
- Reading relevant community discussions.
- Reviewing product feedback.
- Studying search interest.
- Examining existing purchasing behaviour.
- Observing workarounds people already use.

Separate observed evidence from assumptions.

**Expected outcome:** A collection of evidence supporting or challenging
the problem hypothesis.

## 5. Test your proposed solution

Choose the smallest experiment that can test your riskiest assumption.

Examples:

- A landing page explaining the proposed solution.
- A clickable prototype.
- A manual service delivered to a small group.
- A waitlist with a clear value proposition.
- A limited beta with real target users.

Measure meaningful behaviour, not just compliments or page views.

**Expected outcome:** Evidence about whether the proposed solution
attracts genuine interest.

## 6. Evaluate willingness to adopt

Ask whether the intended users would change their existing behaviour.

Consider:

- What would make them switch?
- What would prevent adoption?
- Is the problem important enough to prioritize?
- Would they pay, recommend it or invest time in using it?

Avoid treating hypothetical enthusiasm as proof of purchasing intent.

**Expected outcome:** A clearer understanding of adoption barriers.

## 7. Make a decision

Choose one of three directions:

### Proceed

There is sufficient evidence to justify a small product experiment.

### Iterate

The problem appears meaningful, but the audience, positioning or
solution needs refinement.

### Stop or reconsider

The evidence does not support the current hypothesis.

Changing direction is a useful outcome of validation, not a failure.

## Validation summary

Before committing to a full build, try to establish:

- A clearly defined problem.
- A specific initial audience.
- Evidence of existing demand.
- Knowledge of current alternatives.
- Feedback from relevant users.
- A testable solution hypothesis.
- A measurable next experiment.

## Important note

No checklist or score can guarantee product success. Validation reduces
uncertainty; it does not eliminate market risk.

Use evidence, record assumptions and revisit conclusions as new
information becomes available.
